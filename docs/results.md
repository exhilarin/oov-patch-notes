
# Results and error analysis

All numbers come from `results/*.json`, generated by the scripts in `src/`; the full tables are in
[results/benchmark.md](../results/benchmark.md) and [results/error_analysis.md](../results/error_analysis.md).
Primary metric: strict span-level macro-F1 over 16 change types.

## 1. Main results

| Model | Group | Test macro-F1 | Test boundary-F1 | Unseen-games macro-F1 | Drop on unseen games |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Keyword rules | baseline | 0.123 | 0.454 | 0.152 | +0.03 |
| CRF | baseline | 0.348 | 0.705 | 0.253 | −0.10 |
| BiLSTM-CRF (3 seeds) | baseline | 0.423 ± 0.006 | **0.810** | 0.374 ± 0.009 | −0.05 |
| BERT-base-cased | baseline | 0.496 | 0.605 | 0.445 | −0.05 |
| DeBERTa-v3-base | new | **0.572** | 0.731 | **0.524** | −0.05 |
| ModernBERT-base | new | 0.480 | 0.543 | 0.420 | −0.06 |
| CRF spans + Laya zero-shot | new | 0.158 | 0.720 | 0.142 | −0.02 |
| gold spans + Laya zero-shot* | new | 0.218 | 1.000 | 0.203 | −0.02 |

\* upper bound of the typing step only (Laya rows on a random sample of 500 examples per test split).

**The task is far from saturated.** The best model, DeBERTa-v3, reaches 0.57 span macro-F1 (token
accuracy 0.72); even the most frequent type (FIX) stays at 0.81. This matches what we expected from the data: types are chosen
per section by the developer, and the input has no line breaks or bullets.

## 2. What the numbers say

1. **The new encoder wins clearly.** DeBERTa-v3 is 7.6 points above the BERT baseline on the random
   split (0.572 vs 0.496) and 7.9 points on unseen games (0.524 vs 0.445), even with its word
   embeddings frozen to fit a 4 GB GPU. One possible reason is its disentangled attention over relative
   positions, which suits long, clause-sized spans; we did not test this separately.
2. **Pretrained encoders type best, the CRF layer segments best.** DeBERTa has the best macro-F1,
   but its boundary-F1 is 0.73 and BERT's only 0.60, while BiLSTM-CRF finds 81% of the change
   boundaries exactly. The CRF layer scores whole tag sequences, so a span cannot start with an `I-` tag or
   switch type in the middle; the encoders tag every word on its own, so one wrong tag inside a
   change already breaks the whole span. With good boundaries the encoders would be even better (§4).
3. **Learned features beat hand-made ones.** BiLSTM-CRF, trained from scratch, is 7.5 points above
   the feature-based CRF on the random split and 12 points above it on unseen games. The keyword
   rules show how far simple cues get: 0.12.
4. **New games are harder, but less than expected.** Every trained model loses 5–10 points on the
   190 unseen games. The CRF loses most (−0.10): its features memorise game-specific words. The
   pretrained encoders and the BiLSTM-CRF lose about 5 points.
5. **The newest encoder is not automatically the best.** ModernBERT (2024) stays 1.6 points below
   BERT and 9 points below DeBERTa. It was trained with a smaller batch (2 × 4 accumulation, gradient checkpointing) to fit a
   4 GB GPU, on 20,000 of the 28,272 training examples for 3 epochs; its validation score was still
   rising (0.356 → 0.464 → 0.473), so it is under-trained, not worse by design.
6. **Zero-shot is not enough.** Laya, given only the 16 type definitions, reaches 0.16 with CRF spans
   and only 0.22 even with the gold spans. Typing patch notes needs the developers' conventions,
   which only labelled data teaches.

## 3. Error analysis (CRF, random test split)

**Boundary errors vs type errors.** The CRF finds the exact boundaries of 67.0% of the 48,421 gold
changes, but only 62.9% of those also get the right type. So most of the remaining error is the
type, not the segmentation.

**Which types are confused.** Among changes with correct boundaries, the most frequent confusions
are UI → FIX (24% of UI), GAMEPLAY → FIX (35%), MULTIPLAYER → FIX (35%), GRAPHICS → FIX (27%) and
IMPROVEMENT → FIX / NEW_CONTENT (20% / 18%). The per-type F1 of the best model (DeBERTa) follows
the same pattern: FIX 0.81, KNOWN_ISSUE 0.78, AUDIO 0.71 at the top; IMPROVEMENT 0.36 and
GAMEPLAY 0.35 at the bottom (0.23 each on unseen games).

**Why: headers mix two axes.** Developers name sections either by the **kind** of change
(*Bug Fixes*, *New Content*, *Improvements*) or by the **area** of the game (*UI*, *Audio*,
*Graphics*, *Multiplayer*). An item such as *"Fixed a bug that caused snow to flicker in some
scenes"* is a FIX and a GRAPHICS change at the same time; its label depends only on where the
developer put it. 12.7% of all test items start with a cue word of another type (for example *Fixed …* under
*UI*), most of them under area headers (UI 1,466, GAMEPLAY 801,
GRAPHICS 677). This is real label noise that no model can fully remove and it caps the score.

**Rare types.** ACHIEVEMENT (2,018 items in the whole dataset, 83 times rarer than FIX) and
CONTROLS (3,992) have the lowest support: CONTROLS stays at or below 0.39 span F1 for every
trained model, ACHIEVEMENT at or below 0.45.

## 4. Limitations and next steps

* **One label per item.** The data shows that many items honestly belong to a kind *and* an area.
  A two-layer label set (kind × area) or multi-label spans would remove much of the noise above.
* **Encoder + CRF.** Adding a CRF layer on top of BERT would combine its typing with the
  BiLSTM-CRF's consistent boundaries.
* **Compute.** All encoders were fine-tuned on 20,000 of the 28,272 training examples for 3 epochs
  on a 4 GB laptop GPU; DeBERTa-v3 kept its word embeddings frozen, DeBERTa and ModernBERT used a
  batch of 2 × 4 with gradient checkpointing, and every encoder's validation score was still rising
  after epoch 3, so all of them are under-trained.
* **Popular games only.** The 5,000 most-owned games over-represent large studios.
